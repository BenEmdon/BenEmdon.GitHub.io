$ music-maker#

A small sequencer for 16-bit adventure music, made the way the pixel art on this site is made: the song is text first. Every channel is a little map, one character per step, and the grid below is that same text in another form. Click cells or edit the text; the other view follows, even mid-song.

Click a cell to step it through rest, note, hold, rest. Rows are pitches, high at the top, and the wide gaps fall where the text puts its beat marks. Each channel has a mute of its own.

$ the-song-as-text#

The whole song, exactly as the sequencer reads it. Change anything and it recompiles on the spot. A typo never stops the music: the last good version keeps playing, and the problem shows in plain words above the text.

The alphabet is four characters: 

.  rest
x  a note starts
-  the note holds
|  one beat (four steps)

A block in [brackets] names a channel and its instrument, and can set its volume, its echo send, and its pan. Rows are pitches, or drum names in a drums block. :: A starts a pattern, and an order: line at the end chains the patterns together. With one pattern and no order line, the pattern simply loops.

$ how-it-works#

The instruments are made when the page loads: short sums of sine and saw waves, softened until they sound sampled, because sampled is what the 16-bit consoles played. There is no audio file anywhere in this page, and nothing to download.

Every channel can send into one shared echo, a delay with a dark filter inside its loop. That single echo, on top of the sampled sound, is most of what makes this era of game music feel like itself.

Timing runs on the audio clock, not the screen clock. A timer wakes every 25 milliseconds and places notes a little ahead of the speakers, reading the song as it stands at that moment. That is why an edit lands in the music almost at once.

The idea is Ben's, from a Swift Playground he wrote as a student: a grid of blocks you toggle while the music plays. This is that idea at 16 bits, and taught to read text.
